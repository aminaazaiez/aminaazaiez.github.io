abstract: "This thesis investigates how social interactions shape inequalities of access to political institutions through the lens of higher-order network modeling. Drawing on complex systems science, it investigates how group interactions shape collective organization and individual access to policymaking arenas.  

The first chapter situates the thesis within the literature on political networks. It argues that inequalities of access to political institutions are rooted in patterns of interactions among institutions, individuals, and organizations. While most existing studies focus on dyadic ties, social interactions often unfold in collective settings—community events, deliberative committees, and stakeholder consultations. Capturing these groups interactions requires tools that go beyond traditional graph representations. The chapter thus motivates the use of hypergraphs, where links connect more than two nodes, and asks the following question:**How do higher-order models of group interactions provide new insight into inequalities of access to political institutions?**

The second chapter develops the methodological framework of the research. It provides a unified foundation for the empirical and modeling approaches developed in the following chapters. It formalizes network representations for group interactions and surveys graph and hypergraph metrics.

The third chapter, **Political participation and voluntary associations**, studies face-to-face interactions among members of local associations and elected officials in a French village. The study reveals how social and political participation intertwine. By characterizing the social composition of groups, it introduces a political participation rate and a diversity coefficient, showing how engagement in heterogeneous activities fosters access to local politics.

The fourth chapter, **European Commission lobby network**, examines the structure of meetings between European Commission representatives and stakeholders. It shows that the consultation process exhibits a strong core–periphery structure, reflecting inequalities of access. Comparing hypergraph and dyadic representations reveals that higher-order information uncovers distinct collective lobbying strategies employed by specific categories of stakeholders.

The final chapter,  **Generative models for social group interactions**, develops two models for group interactions that extends the notion of triadic closure to higher-order settings. The models reproduce stylized outcomes of dyadic generative models, such as heterogeneous degree distributions and community formation, while showing that incorporating group interactions quantitatively transforms these patterns. Notably, the models reveal that higher-order interactions leads to the emergence of rich-club ordering among actors, a feature absent in purely dyadic formulations.
